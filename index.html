    Unit 1 · About 60 minutes

    Higher Ed 101

    You've been a college student, so you already know what registration and a syllabus look like from the outside. This unit shows you what's underneath: how a college actually runs, who has authority over what, and why the decisions Ad Astra touches, like scheduling, capacity, and program mix, matter so much to the institutions we serve.

    We start with one student and zoom out, one level at a time, until we're looking at higher education as a whole. The rings in the sidebar show where you are.

    Block A · About 10 minutes

    Curriculum concepts and core vocabulary

    Instead of a list of definitions, we'll follow one student through her degree. Each term builds on the one before it.

    M

    Meet Maya. She's a first-year, full-time Business major who works afternoons at a local bank. She wants to graduate in four years.

    1. 1

      Program of study

      Maya's program of study is the complete set of requirements she has to finish to earn her degree. For a bachelor's degree, that's usually around 120 credit hours, made up of a few parts:

      MajorThe core courses in her field, like accounting, marketing, and management.
      ConcentrationA focus within the major. Maya picks Marketing.
      General educationCourses every student takes across disciplines, like English composition, math, and science.
      ElectivesCourses she chooses freely to fill out the credits she needs.
    2. 2

      Pathway

      A pathway (also called a program map or degree map) lays those requirements out term by term in the order a student should take them to finish on time. Order matters because of prerequisites: Maya can't take Accounting II until she passes Accounting I.

      Maya's first fall semester

      English Composition I (3) College Algebra (3) Intro to Business (3) Principles of Accounting I (3) Intro to Psychology (3)

      MajorGeneral education

    3. 3

      Credit hours

      Each course is worth a number of credit hours, the unit colleges use to measure academic progress. Most of Maya's courses are worth 3.

      Taking 12 or more credit hours a term usually makes a student full-time. But 12 credits a term only gets Maya to 96 credits in four years. To reach 120 on time, she needs about 15 a term. That's what "on pace" means.

      Don't mix up credit hours and contact hours

      Contact hours measure time actually spent in instruction, like class or lab time. Credit hours measure academic credit earned. They often match, but not always.

      CourseCredit hoursContact hours per week (typical)
      Principles of Marketing (lecture)3About 3
      Biology with lab4About 6 (3 lecture + 3 lab)

      The lab course uses far more instructor and room time than its credits suggest, which matters when an institution plans faculty loads and space.

    4. 4

      Courses, sections, and capacity

      In her second year, Maya's pathway calls for Principles of Marketing. That's a course: a subject listed in the catalog, BUS 210. What she actually registers for is a section: a specific offering of that course with its own days, time, room, instructor, and seat limit.

      SectionMeetsRoomEnrolled / cap
      BUS 210-01Mon/Wed/Fri 9:00 a.m.Hall 10430 / 30 full
      BUS 210-02Tue/Thu 11:00 a.m.Hall 212 (48 chairs)35 / 35 full
      BUS 210-03Tue/Thu 2:00 p.m.Hall 11831 / 35 open
      EnrollmentHow many students are registered in a section.
      Seat capacityThe enrollment cap set for a section. Section 02 is capped at 35.
      Room capacityHow many people the physical room holds. Hall 212 has 48 chairs.

      Notice two things. Section 02 shows as full even though its room has 13 empty chairs, because the seat cap, not the room, is the limit. And section 03 has open seats, but it meets while Maya is at work. Seats existing isn't the same as seats a student can actually use.

    5. 5

      Retention, completion, and degree velocity

      Maya can't fit BUS 210 this term, and a later course requires it as a prerequisite. She drops to 12 credits. If that keeps happening, her 120 credits take five years instead of four.

      Degree velocityHow quickly a student moves toward finishing. Maya's has slowed.
      RetentionWhether a student keeps enrolling from one term or year to the next. Every delay raises the risk Maya stops out.
      CompletionWhether a student earns the credential they started. It's the outcome everything else feeds.

      That's the whole chain: one full section affects her pace, her pace affects whether she stays, and whether she stays decides whether she finishes.

    Quick check

    BUS 210-02 shows 35 of 35 students enrolled and meets in a room with 48 chairs. What's keeping Maya out?
    That's it.The section is capped at 35 seats. The room could physically hold more, but the cap is what the registration system enforces.
    Not quite.The room has 13 empty chairs, so the room isn't the limit. The section's seat capacity, its enrollment cap of 35, is.
    Why this matters in your job You can now describe a registration snag in institutional terms. Not just "the class was full," but "every section of a pathway course hit its seat cap, which slows degree velocity." That's the language you'll hear in client conversations.

    Block B · About 15 minutes

    Institutional structure and roles

    We just saw how seat and section availability shapes Maya's path. So who actually decides how many seats and sections exist in the first place?

    The academic hierarchy

    Most colleges have two sides: an academic side that owns curriculum and teaching, and administrative divisions that support students and run operations. Titles vary, but the shape is usually similar.

    • Board of trustees or regents
      • President or chancellor
        • Provost or VP of Academic Affairs
          • Deans of colleges or schools
            • Department chairs
              • Faculty
        • VP of Student Affairs
        • VP of Enrollment Management
        • VP of Finance or CFO
        • Chief Information Officer (IT)

    The Registrar and Scheduling Office usually report to Academic Affairs or Enrollment Management, depending on the institution.

    Who touches the schedule

    Open each office to see its typical role. These vary from campus to campus, but you'll hear all of them in client conversations.

    Academic Affairs

    The provost, deans, and department chairs decide what courses are offered, how many sections, and which faculty teach them. They own the curriculum, including program requirements and pathways.

    Student Affairs

    Supports students outside the classroom. Depending on the institution, this can include advising, disability services, and student success programs. They often see firsthand when students can't get the courses they need.

    Enrollment Management

    Responsible for bringing in and keeping students, often including admissions, financial aid, and sometimes the Registrar. They forecast how many students are coming and what those students will need.

    Registrar

    Keeper of the official academic record and the course catalog. Runs registration, enforces academic policies like prerequisites and holds, and often builds or publishes the schedule.

    Scheduling Office

    Assigns sections to times and rooms and manages how classroom space is used. Sometimes this is its own office; often it sits inside the Registrar's office.

    Finance

    Funds the faculty positions, adjunct budgets, and facilities that make sections possible. Finance doesn't see "a student" the way Maya's story did. It sees FTE.

    FTE (full-time equivalent) converts total credit hours into an equivalent number of full-time students. Remember how a full-time student takes on a certain number of credit hours? Finance has to count that across every student, full-time and part-time.

    StudentCredit hours
    Maya15
    Jordan (part-time)6
    Sam (part-time)9
    Total30

    If 15 credit hours counts as 1 FTE, then 30 credits ÷ 15 = 2 FTE. Three students, but two FTE. The exact formula varies by state and institution, but the idea is the same: funding, staffing, and budgets are often calculated on FTE, not headcount.

    IT

    Runs the student information system (SIS), like Banner, PeopleSoft, Colleague, or Workday Student, where the schedule, registration rules, and student records actually live, along with the tools connected to it.

    Who owns scheduling?

    There's no single answer. Institutions tend to follow one of three models.

    One central office, often in the Registrar's area, builds the schedule and assigns rooms for the whole institution. Departments submit requests; the central office makes the final call.

    Departmentssubmit requests toCentral officewhich builds the full schedule

    Each department or college builds its own schedule and often controls its own rooms. The Registrar mostly collects and publishes what departments decide.

    Each departmentbuilds its own schedule and books its own rooms

    Responsibility is split. A common version: departments decide what to offer and who teaches it, while a central office assigns times and rooms or approves the schedule against shared rules. Many institutions land somewhere in here.

    Departmentsdecide courses and sections;Central officeassigns times and rooms

    One decision, many hands

    A RACI chart shows who's Responsible for doing the work, who's Accountable for the final call, who's Consulted, and who's Informed. Here's one hypothetical hybrid institution adding a new section of BUS 210.

    Where could Maya get blocked?

    Remember: every section of BUS 210 that fits Maya's schedule is full. Select each office to see how it could be part of the reason.

    Quick check

    A college has 300 students, each taking 5 credit hours. If 15 credit hours equals 1 FTE, what's the college's FTE?
    Right.300 students × 5 credits = 1,500 credit hours. 1,500 ÷ 15 = 100 FTE. A headcount of 300, but the funding equivalent of 100 full-time students.
    Not quite.Total the credit hours first (300 × 5 = 1,500), then divide by 15. That gives 100 FTE.
    Why this matters in your job Scheduling authority is usually spread across several offices, and the person you're talking to may own only one piece of it. Knowing who owns, influences, and carries out scheduling decisions helps you understand a client's organization and why a change can take so many conversations.

    Block C · About 15 minutes

    Institution types and funding models

    Zooming out again: a campus isn't always a standalone place, and what pays for it shapes what it prioritizes.

    Common institution types

    Public four-year universitiesIncludes large research universities (often a state's flagship) and regional universities focused on teaching and serving their area. Partly funded by the state.
    Community collegesPublic two-year colleges with open admission. They offer associate degrees, certificates, workforce training, and a starting point for transfer to a four-year school.
    Private nonprofit collegesEverything from small liberal arts colleges to major research universities. Many depend heavily on tuition; some have large endowments.
    For-profit institutionsOwned by companies and run as businesses, with revenue coming mostly from tuition.
    Mission-defined institutionsHistorically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs), tribal colleges, and Hispanic-serving institutions, among others. These can be public or private.

    Systems and districts

    If you went to one campus, you probably thought of it as a standalone school. Many aren't. A system is a group of institutions under a shared governing board, like the State University of New York (SUNY) or the University System of Georgia. A district, common for community colleges, is a group of colleges governed by one board, like Maricopa Community Colleges in Arizona.

    Here's a hypothetical example of what that looks like in practice.

    Prairie Valley Community College District board and district office

    where a student enrolls

    Each college builds its own schedule, but only within the district's rules

    A student at Central College sees one campus. Underneath, the courses Central can offer, the calendar it follows, and the budget it schedules against may all be set above it. How much authority sits at the system level versus the campus level varies a lot, which is exactly why it matters to know which kind of client you're working with.

    Now that we know what kind of institution we're talking about, here's what pays for it.

    Funding models

    Tuition-drivenMost revenue comes from tuition and fees. Common at private colleges.

    Every empty seat and every student who leaves is lost revenue.

    State appropriationThe state legislature allocates funding to public institutions, often based on enrollment or FTE.

    Enrollment counts drive the budget.

    Performance-basedA share of state funding is tied to outcomes like credits completed and degrees awarded.

    Students finishing, not just enrolling, drives the budget.

    Most institutions blend more than one. Performance-based funding is the least intuitive, so here's how it works.

    Example: a hypothetical state funding formula

    A state sets aside part of its higher ed budget and distributes it by results. Each public university earns points for:

    OutcomePoints
    Each student who completes 30 credits in their first year1
    Each bachelor's degree awarded3
    Bonus for each degree earned by a low-income student+1

    Each school's share of the money depends on its share of the points. A university that holds enrollment steady but graduates fewer students can lose funding to a school that graduates more. Several states use some version of this. Tennessee was an early example, tying most of its base funding for public institutions to outcomes.

    Apply it

    If a state ties funding to on-time completion, what do you think the institution starts prioritizing?

    Sample answerMaking sure students can get the courses they need when they need them: enough seats in required and gateway courses, schedules built around pathways rather than faculty preference alone, sections at times working students can attend, and advising that keeps students taking enough credits to stay on pace. In other words, degree velocity becomes a budget issue.
    Why this matters in your job Many registration frustrations trace back to funding and capacity limits, not carelessness. And it's why clients care so much about enrollment and completion numbers: depending on their funding model, those numbers are their budget.

    Block D · About 10 minutes

    Industry pressures: why this matters now

    Maya isn't a rare case. Zoom all the way out and her story is one data point in a pattern across higher education.

    Fewer students, and not enough finishing

    Enrollment. College enrollment nationally declined through much of the 2010s and dropped sharply during the pandemic. It has partially rebounded since, but many institutions are competing for a smaller pool of students, and birth rates fell after the 2008 recession, which means fewer high school graduates in the years ahead.

    Completion. Many students who start college don't finish, and many who do take longer than the "normal" two or four years. Completion rates are commonly measured over 150% of normal time (six years for a bachelor's degree) for exactly that reason, and gaps persist across student groups.

    Question 5

    A student needs a required course to stay on their pathway, but every available section is full. The academic department determines how many sections of the course to offer, while a central scheduling office assigns rooms and manages the schedule. Using terminology from this unit, identify the institution's scheduling model and describe one factor that could be affecting the student's ability to stay on track.

    Sample answerThis is a hybrid model: the department controls how many sections are offered, and a central office controls rooms and times. One factor: the department may not have offered enough sections to meet demand, so every section hit its seat capacity. Others could be no rooms available at times the student can attend, or no budget for another instructor. Any of these slows the student's degree velocity and puts retention and completion at risk.

    Question 6

    Why do enrollment, retention, and completion matter financially and strategically to colleges and universities?

    Sample answerEnrollment drives revenue, whether through tuition or state funding based on FTE. Retention keeps that revenue coming, since replacing a student who leaves is costly. Completion increasingly drives funding directly through performance-based models, and it shapes reputation, rankings, and accountability to the state and accreditors. Strategically, all three show whether the institution is fulfilling its mission.

    Correct.These pressures are industry-wide, not isolated.

    Question 8

    Choose one funding model (tuition-driven, state appropriation, or performance-based) and explain how it would shape an institution's priorities around enrollment or completion.

    Sample answersTuition-driven: every enrolled and retained student is revenue, so the institution focuses on recruiting and keeping students and filling seats. State appropriation: if funding follows enrollment or FTE, growing and holding enrollment protects the budget. Performance-based: funding follows outcomes, so the institution prioritizes on-time completion, like guaranteeing seats in required courses and keeping students on pace.
